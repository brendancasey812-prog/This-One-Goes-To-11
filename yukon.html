Why This Trip Goes to 11

We were fortunate to witness a truly spectacular aurora display on our third night visiting Whitehorse, Canada (the first two nights were too cloudy to see much). The entire sky was swirling and dancing with greens and pinks. It was hard to even know where to look. The show went on for over an hour. Breathtaking!

And on top of that, Whitehorse is a really cute town with lots of other fun things to do. We went dog sledding, toured a wildlife sanctuary full of local animals, and soaked in hot springs surrounded by snow. This trip definitely went to 11! Read on for more details and info.

Things to consider if you want to see the Aurora

There are several places in the world that are ideal for seeing the aurora borealis — Alaska, Canada, Iceland, Sweden, Finland, Norway — places that are above the Arctic Circle in the aurora oval that have dark, clear skies.

We chose Canada because it was new for us and was a relatively short flight and in the same time zone as California. Within Canada there are two main cities for aurora viewing: Yellowknife and Whitehorse. Whitehorse was a lot cheaper, so that's what we picked.

Best timing to catch the Aurora

  • October to March — long, dark nights.
  • No or new moon — less light pollution.
  • 10pm to 2am — it usually happens within an hour or two of local midnight.
  • Close to the autumnal or vernal equinox — increased geomagnetic storms due to the Russell–McPherron effect, for the geeks out there.

We went for the new moon in March closest to the vernal equinox, which maximized our chances of a great aurora while also giving us more daylight and warmer temperatures (only 0°F/−17°C versus −20°F/−29°C). Brrr. Bring LOTS of layers, whenever you go.

Other pro tips for seeing the Aurora

  • Go with a tour. They'll take you to an ideal viewing spot in the middle of the night and keep you warm and entertained while you wait for the magic to start. You don't want to be driving around dark back roads at 1am on your own.
  • Watch the aurora forecast. Yes, there is one — and there are aurora apps. I really like the Alaska Geophysical Institute forecast, which also has a lot of great background on how auroras work.
  • Watch the weather too. You can't see much if it's overcast. It's really disappointing to know the aurora is peaking and all you can see is vaguely green clouds.
  • Go at least 3 nights in a row. We saw a hint of light the first night, a brief show at the very end of the second, and a spectacular display on the third. We almost didn't go out that third night because we had a 5am flight home — then the skies cleared, we got a gorgeous sunset, and we could see it starting from our hotel. So glad we went.

Top 11 things to do in Whitehorse, Yukon, Canada

1

See the Northern Lights

Obviously – this is what you came for, right?!? The Northern Lights (Aurora Borealis) are one of the 7 Natural Wonders of the world for good reason. See my notes about best timing and pro tips for the best ways to guarantee you'll really see them with your own eyes, not just through a camera lens.

Plan to go out for at least 3 nights from 10pm to 2am, ideally with a tour group that can take you to a good viewing spot and provide a warming hut, hot beverages, and a fire when there isn't much to look at.

2

Go dog sledding

We had so much fun with the dogs! The dogs are Alaskan huskies, which are smaller, leaner, and not as fluffy as Siberian huskies. A mixed-breed working dog that is high energy, friendly, and has a strong work ethic. They love to run and pull!

The tour includes time at the beginning and end to give each dog a little love and thank them for the ride. We had a guided tour, which meant that one of us was in a riding pouch behind the dogs while the other stood on the rails behind and the guide was in back making sure we didn't lose control. We would lean to help steer, and step down on a flap between the rails to slow down or brake.

We had 10 dogs on our team. They have lots of trails, so each group went off separately for a private journey through the woods. So fun!

3

See wild animals

Originally a private farm, the Yukon Wildlife Preserve has been owned and operated by the local government since 2004. Wildlife are free to roam in large, fenced-in areas and include bison, caribou, muskox, moose, sheep, deer, goats, elk, foxes, and lynxes.

If you have a car, you can do a self-guided walking tour on a 3 mile (5 km) loop. We opted for the guided driving tour with a driver who stopped at various lookouts and told us about the history of the preserve and the animals.

My favorite was the lynx, of course (I'm a big cat fanatic), but the muskox and moose were also fun. The driver let us hold moose and deer antlers to get a real feel for how heavy they are. And we saw a wild fox running down the road when we were leaving!

4

Relax in natural hot springs

After visiting the Yukon Wildlife Preserve, our tour bus dropped us off at the Eclipse Nordic Hotsprings for a few hours. This facility has an interesting history. The site was originally known as Takhini Hot Springs. It was owned by First Nation Chief Jim Boss (Kishxóot) and was considered a sacred place where hunting was not allowed except during times of scarcity or starvation. It was only accessible by foot. Takhini Hot Springs was developed as a recreational area in the 1940s. The current complex was opened in 2022 as a well center after a 5-year renovation period. The natural mineral water leaves the ground at 45.6℃/117℉ and flows into a series of outdoor pools that range from 37-41℃/98-106℉. Inside are heated relaxation tables as well as wet and dry saunas. Eclipse is famous for its annual hair freezing contest!

5

Learn about Yukon history

The Yukon is famous for the Klondike Gold Rush. 100,000 prospectors attempted to reach the Klondike gold fields from 1896 to 1898. Only 30,000 to 40,000 actually made it. Of these, only around 4,000 struck gold and a few hundred became rich.

Travel conditions were treacherous due to the mountainous terrain and extreme cold. Canadian authorities required "Klondikers" to carry at least a year's worth of food. When combined with their mining and survival gear, a typical Klondiker was carrying up to 1 ton in weight.

Many Klondikers passed through Whitehorse on their quest. The Klondike could only be reached by the Yukon River. River boats came up the Yukon as far as Whitehorse in the summer. After that, prospectors had to travel by foot or using dogs, horses, oxen, or mules to pull their gear.

The MacBride Museum is right downtown and is a great way to spend a few hours out of the cold. It includes a lot of information and artifacts about Yukon First Nations, the natural world, and the Klondike Gold Rush. One room includes a massive grizzly that stands 10 feet tall. Another has a replica of a tent used by gold prospectors. Worth a look.

6

Take a hike

The Whitehorse Millennium Trail is a 3 mile/5 km loop that starts in downtown (turn right at the train station) and travels along the east and west banks of the Yukon River. It is flat and accessible all year. A highlight is passing by the S.S. Klondike, a sternwheeler that ran freight between Whitehorse and Dawson City along the Yukon River in the early 1900's. It has been designated as a National Historic Site of Canada and is open for tours in the summer. In the winter, it is trapped in ice and snow.

7

Explore Main Street

Whitehorse has a really cute main street that is about 5 blocks long. It has a western frontier vibe with lots of gift shops, cafes, and restaurants. We were pleasantly surprised to find that there is a lot of public art, including several statues honoring historical figures (including Jack London, who lived ) as well as several cool murals around town.

8

Enjoy a Caribbean vibe

Yes, Caribbean! There is something really charming about experiencing Antoinette's Restaurant when it is snowing outside. I'm sure it's nice all year, but going in the winter is special. It starts with the bright yellow exterior, and then feeling immediately transported to a tropical island by the colorful interior. The menu features many exotic meat options for the adventurous (wild boar, bison, elk, kangaroo, ostrich, rabbit, frog, ox tail, chicken hearts and pig ears), although it also offers traditional and vegan options.

9

Eat bison ribs

We were surprised to get gourmet food in Whitehorse, but Belly of the Bison inside the Edgewater Hotel offers "elegant comfort food that is upscale but simple." We had seared digby scallops served with mushroom caviar, crispy fry leeks & black truffle oil for an appetizer and braised bison short ribs and mushroom ravioli for our main dishes. Everything was truly outstanding.

10

Choose your own adventure

We used Northern Tales for all of our Whitehorse adventures and were very satisfied. Many of the best adventures (aurora viewing, dog sledding, wildlife preserve, hot springs) are about 30 minutes outside of town. We really appreciated having someone else do all of the driving, especially on the snowy and icy roads and at night.

In addition to the tours described above, there are other activities that might be of interest - like snowmobiling, ice fishing, and snow shoeing. There are also other hiking trails that we didn't have time for.

11

Read Jack London's Call of the Wild and White Fang

Jack London spent time in the area that would become Whitehorse in 1897, during the Klondike Goldrush, when he was 21 years old. His experiences informed the Call of the Wild and White Fang, which describe the gold rush era from the perspective of dogs and wolves.

Read together, the two stories form a circle of life from domesticated dog returning to the wild to half-wolf being domesticated. They give you a new perspective if you go dog sledding. There is also a bronze bust honoring London on the corner of 4th and Main.

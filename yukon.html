Why this trip goes to 11

We were fortunate to witness a truly spectacular aurora display on our third night visiting Whitehorse, Canada (the first two nights were too cloudy to see much). The entire sky was swirling and dancing with greens and pinks. It was hard to even know where to look. The show went on for over an hour. Breathtaking!

And on top of that, Whitehorse is a really cute town with lots of other fun things to do. We went dog sledding, toured a wildlife sanctuary full of local animals, and soaked in hot springs surrounded by snow. This trip definitely went to 11.

Things to consider if you want to see the Aurora

There are several places in the world that are ideal for seeing the aurora borealis — Alaska, Canada, Iceland, Sweden, Finland, Norway — places that sit under the aurora oval and have dark, clear skies. We chose Canada because it was new for us, a relatively short flight, and in the same time zone as California. Within Canada there are two main cities for aurora viewing: Yellowknife and Whitehorse. Whitehorse was a lot cheaper, so that's what we picked.

Best timing to catch the Aurora

  • October to March — long, dark nights.
  • No moon or a new moon — less light pollution.
  • 10pm to 2am — it usually happens within an hour or two of local midnight.
  • Close to the autumnal or vernal equinox — increased geomagnetic storms thanks to the Russell–McPherron effect, for the geeks out there.

We went for the new moon in March closest to the vernal equinox, which maximized our chances of a great aurora while also giving us more daylight and warmer temperatures (only 0°F/−17°C versus −20°F/−29°C). Brrr. Bring LOTS of layers, whenever you go.

Other pro tips for seeing the Aurora

  • Go with a tour. They'll take you to an ideal viewing spot in the middle of the night and keep you warm and entertained while you wait for the magic to start. You don't want to be driving around dark back roads at 1am on your own.
  • Watch the aurora forecast. Yes, there is one — and there are aurora apps. I really like the Alaska Geophysical Institute forecast, which also has a lot of great background on how auroras work.
  • Watch the weather too. You can't see much if it's overcast. It's really disappointing to know the aurora is peaking and all you can see is vaguely green clouds.
  • Go at least 3 nights in a row. We saw a hint of light the first night, a brief show at the very end of the second, and a spectacular display on the third. We almost didn't go out that third night because we had a 5am flight home — then the skies cleared, we got a gorgeous sunset, and we could see it starting from our hotel. So glad we went.

Top 11 Things to Do in Whitehorse, Yukon, Canada

1

See the Northern Lights

Obviously — this is what you came for. The Northern Lights are one of the seven natural wonders of the world for good reason. See my notes on timing and pro tips for the best ways to guarantee you'll really see them with your own eyes, not just through a camera lens.

Plan to go out at least three nights from 10pm to 2am, ideally with a tour group that can take you to a good viewing spot and provide a warming hut, hot drinks, and a fire for when there isn't much to look at.

2

Go dog sledding

We had so much fun with the dogs. They're Alaskan huskies — smaller, leaner, and not as fluffy as Siberian huskies. A mixed-breed working dog that's high energy, friendly, and has a strong work ethic. They love to run and pull.

The tour includes time at the beginning and end to give each dog a little love and thank them for the ride. Ours was guided: one of us rode in a pouch behind the dogs while the other stood on the rails behind, with the guide at the back making sure we didn't lose control. You lean to steer and step on a flap between the rails to slow down or brake.

We had ten dogs on our team. There are lots of trails, so each group goes off separately for a private journey through the woods. So fun.

3

See wild animals

Originally a private farm, the Yukon Wildlife Preserve has been owned and operated by the local government since 2004. Wildlife roam free in large fenced areas: bison, caribou, muskox, moose, sheep, deer, goats, elk, foxes, and lynxes. With a car you can do a self-guided walking tour on a 3 mile (5 km) loop.

We opted for the guided driving tour, where the driver stopped at various lookouts and told us about the history of the preserve and the animals. My favorite was the lynx, of course — I'm a big cat fanatic — but the muskox and moose were also fun. The driver let us hold moose and deer antlers to get a real feel for how heavy they are. And we saw a wild fox running down the road on the way out.

4

Relax in natural hot springs

After the Wildlife Preserve, our tour bus dropped us at Eclipse Nordic Hot Springs for a few hours. The site has an interesting history: it was originally known as Takhini Hot Springs, owned by First Nation Chief Jim Boss (Kishxóot) and considered a sacred place where hunting was not allowed except in times of scarcity or starvation. It was only accessible on foot.

Takhini was developed as a recreational area in the 1940s, and the current complex opened in 2022 after a five-year renovation. The natural mineral water leaves the ground at 45.6°C/117°F and flows into a series of outdoor pools that range from 37–41°C/98–106°F. Inside there are heated relaxation tables and both wet and dry saunas. Eclipse is famous for its annual hair freezing contest.

5

Learn about Yukon history

The MacBride Museum is right downtown and a great way to spend a few hours out of the cold. It holds a lot of information and artifacts about Yukon First Nations, the natural world, and the Klondike Gold Rush. One room includes a massive grizzly that stands ten feet tall; another has a replica of a tent used by gold prospectors. Worth a look.

6

Take a hike

The Whitehorse Millennium Trail is a 3 mile/5 km loop that starts downtown — turn right at the train station — and travels along the east and west banks of the Yukon River. It's flat and accessible all year.

A highlight is passing the S.S. Klondike, a sternwheeler that ran freight between Whitehorse and Dawson City along the Yukon River in the early 1900s. It's a National Historic Site of Canada and is open for tours in summer. In winter, it's trapped in ice and snow.

7

Explore Main Street

Whitehorse has a really cute main street about five blocks long, with a western frontier vibe and lots of gift shops, cafes, and restaurants. We were pleasantly surprised by how much public art there is, including several statues honoring historical figures and some very good murals around town.

8

Enjoy a Caribbean vibe

Yes, Caribbean. There is something really charming about experiencing Antoinette's Restaurant while it's snowing outside. I'm sure it's nice all year, but going in winter is special. It starts with the bright yellow exterior, and then you're immediately transported to a tropical island by the colorful interior.

The menu features many exotic meat options for the adventurous — wild boar, bison, elk, kangaroo, ostrich, rabbit, frog, ox tail, chicken hearts, pig ears — although it also offers traditional and vegan options.

9

Eat bison ribs

We were surprised to find gourmet food in Whitehorse, but Belly of the Bison, inside the Edgewater Hotel, offers "elegant comfort food that is upscale but simple." We had seared Digby scallops with mushroom caviar, crispy fried leeks, and black truffle oil to start, then braised bison short ribs and mushroom ravioli for mains. Everything was truly outstanding.

10

Choose your own adventure

Beyond the tours above, there are other activities that might interest you: snowmobiling, ice fishing, and snowshoeing. There are also other hiking trails we didn't have time for.

11

Read Jack London's Call of the Wild and White Fang

Jack London spent time in the area that would become Whitehorse in 1897, during the Klondike Gold Rush, when he was 21 years old. His experiences informed The Call of the Wild and White Fang, which describe the gold rush era from the perspective of dogs and wolves.

Read together, the two stories form a circle of life — domesticated dog returning to the wild, half-wolf being domesticated. They give you a new perspective if you go dog sledding. There's also a bronze bust honoring London on the corner of 4th and Main.

9-Day Summer Itinerary

Norway in summer is the land of the midnight sun. On the solstice the sun "sets" around 10:45pm and rises around 4:00am — but honestly, it only really gets dark for a few hours in the middle of the night. That leaves a lot of daylight for adventures. Bring an eyemask.

Why This Trip Goes to 11

My husband and I are in our early 60s, and we did this trip with our two kids in their early 20s to celebrate our daughter's college graduation. We all agreed that this itinerary was the perfect balance of urban sites and amazing scenery — and the food was remarkably outstanding. (In contrast to, say, Iceland, where we had a running joke about whether Arctic char would be one of the three things on the menu.)

We decided to rent a car to maximize our flexibility, and stayed two nights each in Oslo, Flåm, Bergen, and Stavanger, with a detour at the end for lunch at an underwater, Michelin-starred restaurant at the southernmost tip of the country — more about that later. It was about 19 hours of driving over 9 days, which was about right. We definitely wouldn't have wanted to spend any more time in the car than we did.

The weather in summer is generally very pleasant, with highs of 25°C/77°F and lows to 13°C/55°F. It does rain a fair amount, so pack layers including rain gear.

Driving in Norway

Driving in Norway is a breeze. The roads are well marked and the drivers are polite. Even the ferries are easy: get in line, wait until someone waves you forward, drive on board, get out of your car, and enjoy the boat ride.

Oslo to Flåm — about 5 hours

You have the option of taking the "snow road" along the top of the mountains, or going through the Lærdal Tunnel (E16), the longest tunnel in the world at 15 miles. Although the snow road can have epic views through snow and ice, we read that (1) it's often overcast, so you can't see much of anything, and (2) most of the snow and ice has melted by mid-summer. So we opted for the world's longest tunnel.

To make the tunnel more interesting for drivers, there are signposts telling you how far you've gone, plus trippy light breaks every few miles where the tunnel is lit up in different colors.

We stopped at both the Borgund Stave Church and the Stegastein Viewpoint on the drive to avoid back-tracking. Both were great stops. The Borgund Stave Church was built around 1200 and is the best preserved of the 28 remaining stave churches in Norway. Stegastein is stop number 4 below.

Flåm to Bergen — about 2.5 hours

Short enough that there's time for a hike along the way. We did the Brekkefossen waterfall hike, about 5 km (3 miles) round trip. It includes 578 stone steps built by Nepalese sherpas and ends with spectacular views of the waterfall and the valley down to the Aurlandsfjord.

Bergen to Stavanger — about 4.5 hours

There are several ferries along the way that break up the trip nicely.

Stavanger back to Oslo — about 7 hours

The longest leg. If you add a stop for lunch at Under, it's 8 hours of driving.

Read the road signs

A blue square with a white M indicates a passing place (møteplass) — a widened section on narrow, single-lane roads where two cars can meet and pass. You'll want these on the way up to Stegastein.

Why everything is electric

Most of Norway's electricity comes from hydropower — not surprising when you drive around and see incredible waterfalls at every corner. Norway also leads the world in electric vehicle adoption, with 96% of new car registrations fully electric, and a massive public charging network to support it.

The Cities

Oslo

The capital · ~750,000 people

Norway's capital and largest city — similar in size and feel to Seattle. It's fun, walkable, and definitely worth a day or two. Oslo is known for housing Edvard Munch's The Scream, hosting the Nobel Peace Prize, and blending modern architecture with natural beauty.

There's a lovely promenade along the waterfront. Definitely stroll the rooftop of the Opera House, which sits next to the Munch Museum and has a cool view of the city and harbor.

Flåm

Very small · very touristy

Flåm's main reason for existence is that it's the starting point for two of Norway's biggest attractions — the Nærøyfjord and the Flåm Railway. The spectacular Stegastein Viewpoint and Borgund Stave Church are also just up the road. Massive cruise ships arrive daily.

We stayed at the Svingen Guesthouse, which had an amazing view and was walking distance to the main attractions, but it was quite pricey and has only nine rooms with shared bathrooms. We probably would have preferred one of the nearby towns like Aurland. Lodging is very limited around Flåm — if you want to stay overnight, book early.

Bergen

Gateway to the fjords

Norway's second largest city, known as the "Gateway to the Fjords" because it sits halfway between two of the most iconic ones — the Sognefjord (which includes the Nærøyfjord) to the north and the Hardangerfjord to the south.

Bergen is also home to the UNESCO-listed neighborhood of Bryggen, a major trading village in the Middle Ages. The entire city burned in 1702, and the main street across from the harbor is still lined with the colorful wooden buildings put up after the fire.

Stavanger

The energy capital

Known as Norway's energy capital after oil was discovered off its coast in the North Sea in 1969. Norway remains one of the world's largest oil exporters, which has made it one of the richest and most prosperous countries in the world.

Stavanger has a very international feel, with restaurants from all over the world and multiple Michelin-starred options. It's also the starting point for the Preikestolen (Pulpit Rock) hike and Lysefjord cruises.

Top 11 Summer Stops in Southwestern Norway

1

Check out The Scream (Oslo)

The MUNCH Museum is very impressive. Architecturally it sits right on the waterfront with the upper part of the building angled to the side, and the exhibits are well-organized across multiple floors.

The main collection is on the fourth floor. A small room in the center houses three versions of The Scream, each hidden behind a sliding door to protect it from light exposure and visible for one hour at a time. At the top of the hour one door closes and another opens, so there's always one on display — we were lucky enough to see two by arriving just before the change. The sixth floor has some of Munch's larger pieces, which take up entire walls.

Timed entry NOK 280 (~$30 USD) for adults, reduced after 6pm. Under 18 free; 18–25 NOK 100 (~$10 USD).

2

Explore Grünerløkka (Oslo)

Our Airbnb was in the Grünerløkka neighborhood, which we really enjoyed. Historically this was Oslo's working-class district, built up during industrialization in the second half of the 1800s. Today Løkka is more about art, food, and culture.

We enjoyed exploring the Botanical Garden and the restaurants and bars along the Akerselva River. Grünerløkka is a short walk or a quick tram ride from the waterfront.

3

Board the Fram (Oslo)

The Fram Museum is dedicated to the Fram, a ship commissioned by Fridtjof Nansen and built specifically for polar exploration. His idea was to deliberately get the ship stuck in the Arctic ice, drift over the North Pole, and cover the final distance by dogsled.

It almost worked — they were gone for three years but never quite reached the pole. Fortunately everyone survived, including the ship, which you can now walk aboard.

Tickets NOK 180 (~$19 USD) for adults, with discounts for kids, seniors, and students.

4

Take in the Stegastein View (Flåm)

About 18 km (11 miles) from Flåm, the Stegastein Viewpoint is truly jaw-dropping. You have to drive up a loooooooooooooong single-lane road packed with other cars and tour buses, with crazy switchbacks as you climb.

And then you get to the lookout: a platform that juts out into the middle of nowhere with a sheer drop and spectacular views in both directions.

5

Cruise the Nærøyfjord (Flåm)

Definitely one of the highlights of our trip. It's a two-hour ride through a UNESCO World Heritage site with steep green slopes, amazing waterfalls, and adorable villages along the way.

The boat itself is really cool — fully electric, silent, beautifully designed, with viewing platforms on the front and back and walkways along the sides so there's plenty of room for everyone. And if the weather gets wet or cold, you can retreat inside for a drink or snack.

The cruise ends in the town of Gudvangen, home to the Viking Village of Njardarheimr. We enjoyed our time here — lots of little huts where people dressed as vikings will talk to you or show you their culture. We tried on some viking gear, threw an ax, and watched a smithy. The gift shop was surprisingly good.

It's a 20-minute bus ride back to Flåm, and best to book the boat and bus round trip in advance: NOK 640 (~$70 USD) round trip, plus NOK 260 (~$28 USD) to add the Viking Village.

6

Ride the Flåm Railway (Flåm)

The Flåm Railway is touted as one of the most iconic Norwegian activities — part of "Norway in a Nutshell" — and has been named one of the most scenic train journeys in the world. However, we were underwhelmed.

Things that were cool

  • It's one of the steepest standard-gauge railways in the world.
  • The line includes 20 tunnels, one with a 180-degree turn inside the mountain.
  • It passes through steep mountains and rushing waterfalls, including the majestic Kjosfossen.

Things that were less cool

  • It only takes an hour.
  • There are hordes of people and a mad rush for seats. If you don't get one near a window, it's hard to see the scenery.
  • Did I mention 20 tunnels? It's hard to see anything when you're inside a tunnel.
  • Did I mention Kjosfossen? The train stops for five minutes so a red-dressed huldra can appear and dance to piped-in music. Sooooo cringy.

There are several ways to get back down:

  • Train round trip — NOK 675 (~$75 USD)
  • Train up, then hike and bike down — NOK 913 (~$98 USD)
  • Train up, then zipline and bike down — NOK 1,649 (~$175 USD)

We opted to hike and bike down, which was fun. The hiking part is 3.5 km (2.1 miles) and includes 21 hairpin turns, several waterfalls, and a lot fewer tourists. The biking is 17 km (10.5 miles) and is mostly flat or downhill. We were glad we brought waterproof gear — it rained a fair amount on the way down.

7

Stroll through Bryggen (Bergen)

Bryggen is the historic downtown district in Bergen. The main street along the wharf is lined with colorful wooden buildings whose foundations go back to the 12th century and which have been rebuilt many times after fires. Bryggen is on UNESCO's World Heritage List, and Bergen is a World Heritage City.

Behind the colorful buildings are alleyways with artist galleries and artisan shops. There's also a fantastic fish market and amazing restaurants along the water. We had one of our best meals here.

8

Ride the Fløibanen funicular (Bergen)

One of the top attractions in Bergen. The funicular leaves from downtown and travels to the top of Mount Fløyen in about six minutes. There's a line, but it moves quickly.

At the top you're rewarded with panoramic views of Bergen and the surrounding areas. There's also a large network of walking and hiking trails, a playground, goats roaming around, and a Troll Forest. It's easy to get away from the crowds by strolling along one of the many paths.

Some people walk down through town rather than taking the funicular back. Another option is the Vidden trail between Mount Fløyen and Mount Ulriken — 15 km (9 miles), about 5 hours. Most people take the Ulriken cable car up, hike to Fløyen, and take the funicular down.

Round trip NOK 220 (~$25 USD) onsite, with a small discount for booking in advance.

9

Hike Preikestolen (Pulpit's Rock) (Stavanger)

OH YES. One of my all-time favorite hikes. 8 km (5 miles) round trip with 500 m (1,640 ft) of elevation gain. Very crowded — similar to hiking the Mist Trail in Yosemite — but worth it.

The trail starts by meandering through wooded areas with boardwalks across marshland and then starts climbing. There are a lot of stone stairs and some sections where you need to scramble a bit, but it's considered moderately strenuous.

And then the summit is spectacular. Preikestolen juts out over the Lysefjord with breathtaking views in both directions. Bring a lunch and enjoy the view as long as you can.

10

Dine in Pedersgata (Stavanger)

Pedersgata has become "restaurant row" in Stavanger, with multiple Michelin-starred restaurants nestled on a small street of colorful, restored wooden buildings.

The cuisine is very international — Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese, Thai, Indian/Pakistani, Middle Eastern, Italian, and Spanish — ranging from casual to fine dining. We enjoyed MaTsaYa Bobas and BBQ.

11

Eat at Under (Lindesnes)

We took a little detour on the way from Stavanger back to Oslo to have lunch at Under, an underwater Michelin-starred restaurant, and we were so glad we did. You enter above water and walk down a concrete staircase to a massive underwater window — 11 m (36 ft) wide, 3.4 m (11 ft) high, and 26.5 cm (10.4 in) thick.

All the tables — about 15 in total — face the window, so you watch sea life float and swim past while you dine.

We had a six-course fixed-price lunch with juice pairing. Everything was locally sourced and beautifully prepared and presented, with exceptional service. I really appreciated that each dish had an incredibly complex flavor profile complemented by texture differences — little bits of crunch. Yum.

What it costs

  • Lunch: 6 courses, NOK 1,650 (~$175 USD) · juice pairing NOK 750 (~$80) · wine pairing NOK 1,250 (~$135)
  • Dinner: 10–12 courses, NOK 2,750 (~$300 USD) · juice pairing NOK 1,250 (~$135) · wine pairing NOK 2,250 (~$242)

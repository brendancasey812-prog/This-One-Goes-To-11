Perfect Norway
9-Day Summer Itinerary

Norway in summer is the land of the midnight sun. On the summer solstice, the sun "sets" at around 10:45pm and rises around 4:00am, but honestly, it really only gets "dark" for a few hours in the middle of the night. This leaves a lot of daylight for adventures. But also be sure to bring an eyemask to help with sleeping at night!

Why This Trip Goes to 11

My husband and I are in our early 60's, and we did this trip with our two kids in their early 20's to celebrate our daughter's college graduation. We all agreed that this itinerary was the perfect balance of urban sites and amazing scenery, and the food was remarkably outstanding (in contrast to, say, Iceland, where we had a running joke about whether Arctic Char would be one of the three things on the menu).

We decided to rent a car to maximize our flexibility. We stayed two nights each in Oslo, Flåm, Bergen, and Stavanger, with a detour at the end to have lunch at an underwater, Michelin-starred restaurant at the southernmost tip of the country (more about that later).

Here's an interactive map of our tour. It was about 19 total hours of driving over 9 days, which was about right. We definitely wouldn't have wanted to spend any more time driving than we did.

The weather in the summer is generally very pleasant, with highs of 25℃/77℉ and lows to 13℃/55℉. However, it does rain a fair amount, so it's wise to pack layers including rain gear.

Driving in Norway

It really is a breeze

The roads are well marked, and the drivers are polite. Even the ferries are easy to navigate — just get in line, wait until someone waves you forward, drive on board, get out of your car and enjoy the boat ride.

Read the road signs

A blue square with a white M indicates a passing place (møteplass), marking a widened section on narrow, single-lane roads where two cars can meet and pass.

Below are my Top 11 Stops, organized by city.

Stops 1–3 · Two nights

Oslo

Oslo is Norway's capital and largest city. With about 750,000 residents, it's similar in size and feel to Seattle. It is a fun, walkable city, and definitely worth spending a day or two. Oslo is known for housing Edvard Munch's The Scream, hosting the Nobel Peace Prize, and blending modern architecture with natural beauty. It has a lovely promenade along the waterfront. Definitely stroll the rooftop of the Opera House, which is next to the Munch Museum and has a cool view of the city and harbor.

1

Check out The Scream (Oslo)

The MUNCH Museum was very impressive. Architecturally, it sits right on the waterfront with the upper part of the building angled to the side. The exhibits are very well-organized on multiple floors.

The main collection is on the fourth floor. There is a small room in the center that houses three versions of Munch's iconic painting, The Scream. Each one is hidden behind a sliding door to protect it from light exposure and is visible for one hour at a time.

At the top of the hour, one door closes and another opens, so there is always one version on display. We were fortunate to see two of them by arriving just before the change. The sixth floor includes some of Munch's larger pieces, which take up entire walls.

The timed entrance fee is NOK 280 ($30 USD) for adults, with reduced prices after 6pm. Kids under 18 are free, and young adults aged 18 to 25 are NOK 100 ($10 USD).

2

Explore Grünerløkka (Oslo)

Our AirBnB in Oslo was in the Grünerløkka neighborhood, which we really enjoyed. Historically, Grünerløkka was the working-class district of Oslo that was built up during the industrialization era in the second half of the 1800s. Today, Løkka is more about art, food, and culture.

We enjoyed exploring the Botanical Garden and the restaurants and bars along the Akerselva River. The photo is from the BLÅ Bar, where we watched a few World Cup games. Grünerløkka is a short walk or a quick tram ride from the waterfront.

3

Board the Fram (Oslo)

The Fram museum is dedicated to The Fram, a ship commissioned by Fridtjof Nansen and built specifically for polar exploration. He built the ship purposefully to survive getting stuck in the polar ice — his idea was to get the ship stuck in the Arctic ice and then drift over the North Pole and cover the final distance in dogsleds.

It almost worked — they were gone for three years but never quite reached the North Pole. Fortunately everyone survived — including the ship, which you can now visit.

Tickets are NOK 180 for adults (about $19 USD) with discounts for kids, seniors, and students.

On the road

Oslo to Flåm — about 5 hours

You have the option of taking the "snow road," which travels along the top of the mountains, or going through the Lærdal Tunnel (E16), which is the longest tunnel in the world (15 miles). Although the snow road can have epic views through snow and ice, we read that 1) it is often overcast, so you can't see much of anything, and 2) most of the snow and ice has melted by mid-summer. So we opted for the world's longest tunnel.

To make the tunnel more interesting for drivers, they have signposts telling you how far you've gone as well as trippy light breaks every few miles in which the tunnel is lit up in different colors.

As we discovered during our trip, there are a LOT of really long tunnels in Norway, many of which have this type of lighting, so it is probably not worth going out of your way to drive through it.

We stopped at both the Borgund Stave Church and the Stegastein Viewpoint on our drive from Oslo to Flåm to avoid back-tracking. Both were great stops. The Borgund Stave Church was built around 1200 and is the best preserved of the 28 remaining stave churches in Norway. Stegastein is described below in Top Spot #4.

Stops 4–6 · Two nights

Flåm

Flåm is a very small, very touristy town. Its main reason for existence is that it serves as the starting point for two of Norway's biggest attractions — the Nærøyfjord and the Flåmsbana Railway — both described in more detail below. The spectacular Stegastein Viewpoint and Borgund Stave Church are also just up the road.

Massive cruise ships arrive daily. We stayed in the Svingen Guesthouse in Flåm, which had an amazing view and was walking distance to the main attractions, but it was quite pricey, and it only has 9 rooms with shared bathrooms. We probably would have preferred one of the other nearby towns like Aurland. Lodging is very limited around Flåm, so if you want to stay overnight, book early!

4

Take in the Stegastein View (Flåm)

About 18 km (11 miles) from Flåm, the Stegastein Viewpoint is truly jaw-dropping. You have to drive up a loooooooooooooong, single-lane road packed with other cars and tour buses, with crazy switchbacks as you climb. And then you get to the lookout, which is a platform that juts out into the middle of nowhere with a sheer drop and spectacular views in both directions. This video gives a sense of the drama.

5

Cruise the Nærøyfjord (Flåm)

The Nærøyfjord cruise was definitely one of the highlights of our trip. It is a 2-hour ride through a UNESCO World Heritage site with steep green slopes, amazing waterfalls, and adorable villages along the way.

The boat itself is really cool — fully electric, silent, beautifully designed with viewing platforms on the front and the back and walkways along the sides so there is plenty of room for everyone. And if the weather gets wet or cold, you can retreat inside for a drink or snack.

The cruise ends in the town of Gudvangen, which is home to the Viking Village of Njardarheimr. We enjoyed our time here. There are lots of little huts where people are dressed as vikings and will talk to you or show you their culture. We tried on some viking gear, threw an ax, and watched a smithy. The gift shop was surprisingly good.

It is a 20-minute bus ride back to Flåm and best to book the bus and boat round trip in advance. The cost is 640 NOK (~$70 USD) for the roundtrip, plus 260 NOK ($28 USD) to add the Viking Village.

6

Ride the Flåm Railway (Flåm)

The Flåm Railway is touted as one of the most iconic Norwegian activities (part of Norway in a Nutshell) and has been named one of the most scenic train journeys in the world. However, we were underwhelmed.

Things that were cool

  • It is one of the steepest standard-gauge railways in the world.
  • The line includes 20 tunnels, one of which features a 180-degree turn inside the mountain.
  • It passes through steep mountains and rushing waterfalls, including the majestic Kjosfossen waterfall.

Things that were less cool

  • It only takes 1 hour.
  • There are hordes of people and a mad rush to get seats. If you don't get a seat near the window, it is hard to see the scenery.
  • Did I mention 20 tunnels? It's hard to see anything when you are inside a tunnel…
  • Did I mention the Kjosfossen waterfall? The train stops there for 5 minutes to allow for a red-dressed "huldra" to appear and dance to piped in music… sooooo cringy…

There are several ways to get down:

  • You can take the train round trip (675 NOK, $75 USD)
  • You can take the train up and then hike and bike down (913 NOK, $98 USD)
  • You can take the train up and zipline and bike down (1,649 NOK, $175 USD)

We opted to hike and bike down, which was fun. The hiking part is 3.5 km (2.1 miles) and includes 21 hairpin turns, several waterfalls, and a lot fewer tourists. The biking is 17 km (10.5 miles) and is mostly flat or downhill. We were glad we brought our waterproof gear as it rained a fair amount on the way down.

On the road

Flåm to Bergen — about 2.5 hours

The drive is short, so there is time for a hike along the way. We did the Brekkefossen waterfall hike, which is about 5 km (3 miles) round trip. The hike includes 578 stone steps built by Nepalese sherpas and ends with spectacular views of the waterfall and the valley to the Aurlandsfjord.

Stops 7–8 · Two nights

Bergen

Bergen is Norway's 2nd largest city. It is known as the "Gateway to the Fjords" because it sits halfway in between two of Norway's most-iconic fjords — the Sognefjord (which includes the Nærøyfjord) to the north and Hardangerfjord to the south. In addition, Bergen is home to the UNESCO-listed World Heritage neighborhood of Bryggen.

Bryggen was a major trading village in the Middle Ages. The entire city burned in 1702, and the main street across from the harbor is still lined with the colorful, wooden buildings that were built after the fire.

7

Stroll through Bryggen (Bergen)

As described above, Bryggen is the historic downtown district in Bergen. The main street along the wharf is lined with colorful wooden buildings. Their foundations go back to the 12th century and have been rebuilt many times after fires. Bryggen is on UNESCO's World Heritage List, and Bergen is a World Heritage City.

Behind the colorful buildings are alleyways with artist galleries and artisan shops. There is also a fantastic fish market and amazing restaurants along the water. We had one of our best meals here.

8

Ride the Fløibanen funicular (Bergen)

One of the top attractions in Bergen is the Fløibanen funicular to the top of Mount Fløyen. The funicular leaves from downtown and travels to the top of the mountain in about 6 minutes. There is a line, but it moves quickly.

At the top, you are rewarded with panoramic views of Bergen and the surrounding areas. There is also a large network of walking and hiking trails, a playground area, goats roaming around, and a Troll Forest. It is easy to get away from the crowds by strolling along one of the many paths.

Some people choose to walk down through town rather than taking the funicular back down. Another option is to hike the Vidden trail between Mount Fløyen and Mount Ulriken, which is 15 km (9 miles) and takes about 5 hours. Most people take the Ulriken cable car to the top of Mount Ulriken and then hike to Mount Fløyen and take the Fløibanen funicular back down.

Round trip tickets are NOK 220 ($25 USD) if purchased onsite, or you can purchase in advance for a small discount.

Stops 9–10 · Two nights

Stavanger

The drive from Bergen to Stavanger is about 4.5 hours. There are several ferries along the way that break up the trip.

Stavanger is known as Norway's energy capital due to the discovery of oil off its coast in the North Sea in 1969. Norway continues to be one of the world's largest exporters of oil, which has made it one of the richest and most prosperous countries in the world.

Interestingly, most of the electricity in Norway comes from hydropower — which is not surprising when you drive around and see incredible waterfalls around every corner. Norway also leads the world in electric vehicle adoption, with 96% of new car registrations being fully electric. A massive public charging network is available.

Stavanger has a very international feel, with restaurants featuring cuisine from all over the world and multiple Michelin-starred options. It is also the starting point for the Preikestolen (Pulpit's Rock) hike and Lysefjord cruises.

9

Hike Preikestolen (Pulpit's Rock) (Stavanger)

OH YES!!! One of my all-time favorite hikes. 8 km (5 mi) roundtrip with 500 m (1,640 ft) elevation gain. Very crowded (similar to hiking the Mist Trail in Yosemite), but worth it!

The trail itself starts by meandering through some wooded areas with boardwalks across marshlands and then starts climbing. There are a lot of stone stairs and areas where you need to scramble a bit, but it is doable for those who are reasonably fit.

And then the summit is spectacular. Preikestolen itself juts out over the Lysefjord with breathtaking views in both directions. Get an early start, bring a lunch, and enjoy the view as long as you can.

10

Dine in Pedersgata (Stavanger)

Pedersgata has become "restaurant row" in Stavanger. It features multiple Michelin-starred restaurants nestled on a small street with colorful, restored wooden buildings.

The cuisine is very international and includes Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese, Thai, Indian/Pakistani, Middle Eastern, Italian, and Spanish offerings that range from casual to fine dining experiences. We enjoyed MaTsaYa Bobas and BBQ.

11

Eat at Under (Lindesnes)

The last leg from Stavanger back to Oslo is the longest — 7 hours. We took a little detour to have lunch at Under that added about an hour to the drive, and we were so glad we did!

Under is an under-water, Michelin-starred restaurant in Lindesnes, the southern-most tip of Norway.

You enter a non-descript concrete building above water and then walk down a concrete staircase that brings you to a massive underwater window that is 11 m (36 ft) wide, 3.4 m (11 ft) high, and 26.5 cm (10.4 in) thick.

The entire space has an ethereal blue lighting. It feels calm and quiet, like you are floating. All the tables (about 15 total) face the window, so you can watch the sea life float and swim by while you dine.

We had a 6-course fixed price lunch menu with juice pairing. Everything was locally sourced and beautifully prepared and presented with exceptional service. I really appreciated that each dish had an incredibly complex flavor profile that was complemented by texture differences (little bits of crunch). Yum!!!

What it costs

  • Lunch: 6 courses, NOK 1,650 ($175 USD) · juice pairing NOK 750 ($80) · wine pairing NOK 1,250 ($135)
  • Dinner: 10–12 courses, NOK 2,750 ($300 USD) · wine pairing NOK 2,250 ($242) · juice pairing NOK 1,250 ($135)
